At a glance

DepthWizard in six facts

Input

One RGB image: PNG, JPG or GeoTIFF. Satellite, aerial or drone. Optional: a DEM file, ground control points.

Output

A digital surface model as a Float32 GeoTIFF: metres above sea level (EGM2008) for georeferenced images, relative heights (0 to 1) for plain photos.

Core idea

A 30 m DEM supplies the terrain. A depth network supplies the detail the DEM can't see. The DEM also sets the network's scale.

over $all_b_tiles LiDAR test tiles (urban, sparse, hilly, forested). The DEM alone: $all_dem_rmse m. Correlation $all_dem_r → $all_b_r.

${rt_depth} s

Delivery

A desktop app (Tauri + bundled Python backend) with a Three.js flythrough viewer. Also runs as a local web app. Fully offline.

1 · The problem

Height is the missing dimension in most imagery

A satellite image shows where things are. It doesn't show how tall they are. Yet many decisions depend on height: whether floodwater will reach a house, whether a new tower blocks a radio link, how much shade falls on a roof, how steep a hillside is before a road is cut into it.

How elevation is measured today

MethodHow it worksLimits
Stereo photogrammetryTwo or more images of the same place from different angles; height comes from the parallax between them. CartoDEM was made this way from Cartosat-1 stereo pairs.Needs a stereo pair taken close in time, careful image matching and specialist software. Struggles in shadows and on uniform surfaces.
Airborne LiDARA laser scanner on an aircraft or drone measures distances directly, many points per square metre.The most accurate option, but every survey is a flight campaign with permissions, cost and processing. Coverage is patchy and goes out of date.
Radar interferometry (InSAR)Phase differences between radar acquisitions. SRTM and TanDEM-X were made this way.Global, but the free products are posted at about 30 m. Individual buildings aren't resolved, and processing is heavy.
Global DEMs (SRTM, Copernicus GLO-30, CartoDEM)Ready-made, free elevation grids at about 30 m.A 30 m cell is wider than most buildings. Hills and valleys are there; buildings, trees and streets are not.

Why a single image is hard

Height from one image is ill-posed: many surfaces can produce the same picture. Depth networks learn strong priors from millions of photos, but three things get in the way for satellite imagery:

  • The viewpoint is unusual. The networks are trained mostly on ground-level photos, where the camera looks across a scene. A satellite looks straight down.
  • The output has no units. The network says what is nearer and what is farther, not by how many metres.
  • The network doesn't know where the image is. It can't tie heights to sea level or to the ground around them.

What ISRO asked for

Problem statement SIH26175 asks for a pipeline that:

  1. takes one RGB image (PNG, JPG or GeoTIFF) and outputs a DSM in a standard geospatial format;
  2. gives a relative DSM for images without location data;
  3. gives an absolute DSM in metres for georeferenced images, scaled with a coarser DEM such as SRTM, or GCPs;
  4. drapes the image over a 3D mesh, with first-person navigation and tools to check heights and slopes;
  5. lets users upload imagery and compare estimated heights with reference data;
  6. ships as a standalone application with full source and documentation.

Scoring: 50 % DSM accuracy (RMSE, MAE and correlation against LiDAR, stable across urban, sparse, hilly and forested land); 50 % visualisation (projection accuracy, visual quality, navigation, interface, stability, standalone deployment).

2 · Why we need it

India already has the ingredients. The missing piece is building-level height.

ISRO's Cartosat satellites image the country at metre and sub-metre resolution, and NRSC distributes CartoDEM, a national 30 m elevation model, through Bhuvan. Between the two sits a gap: the DEM can't see a house, and a single image doesn't measure one. DepthWizard fills that gap with software, using data that already exists.

Coverage

High-resolution surface models from LiDAR or stereo exist only where a survey was flown or a stereo pair was acquired, and they age as towns grow. A single new image is usually the first, and often the only, fresh data after a change on the ground.

Speed

After a flood, landslide or earthquake, responders need a current picture of what stands where. A single-image height model can be produced minutes after the image arrives. Planning a new LiDAR flight or stereo acquisition takes far longer.

Cost and skills

Photogrammetric processing needs trained staff and specialist software. DepthWizard needs one image, a free DEM and a laptop with a gaming-class GPU. The whole pipeline is a single button.

Hill and mountain towns

In the Himalaya, the North-East and the Western Ghats, towns sit on steep slopes. There the DEM gets the mountain right and the buildings wrong. That's the case DepthWizard is built for: terrain from the DEM, detail from the image.

"The DEM gives us the terrain. The model gives us what a 30 m DEM can't see."

This is the design principle behind every part of the pipeline. The network is never trusted with the big shapes (hills, valleys, the height of the whole scene). It only adds local detail on top of a DEM, and the DEM decides how much.

Why not just use the depth network?

Because its metres are invented. A raw network output stretched to the DEM's range (our "Method A" baseline) gets hills wrong, because the network confuses slope with height. On our test tiles it is several times worse than simply using the DEM (appendix D). Keeping the DEM's terrain and adding only calibrated detail (our "Method B") is what makes the result usable.

Why not just use the DEM?

Because the DEM is the baseline to beat, and we report it everywhere. A 30 m DEM already captures most of the terrain height, so any honest method must show what it adds on top: sharper buildings, tree crowns, street canyons, and a better match to LiDAR where those features matter. Section 9 shows exactly that, landscape by landscape, including where we add nothing.

4 · What it contributes

Impact and value

  1. It makes existing assets go further. Any georeferenced optical image plus CartoDEM (or Copernicus GLO-30, or SRTM) becomes a building-level surface model. No new sensor, no new flight, no stereo pair.
  2. It's honest by design. Every output records where its metres came from: the calibration method, a confidence level, the DEM and its vertical datum. Plain images without a location get a relative model; we never invent metres. The evaluation always shows the DEM-only baseline next to our result, and our worst tiles next to our best.
  3. It's fast and cheap. ${rt_depth} s of GPU time for relative height and ${rt_cal} s to calibrate a $rt_scene_px px scene on an 8 GB laptop GPU. The same code runs on a CPU when there's no GPU.
  4. It's offline and self-contained. The desktop app bundles the model, the backend and the viewer. No internet, no cloud upload, no account. The only optional network use is a one-time download of the Copernicus DEM tile for a new area.
  5. It uses standard formats. The DSM is a Float32 GeoTIFF with calibration tags, so it opens in QGIS, ArcGIS or any GDAL tool and can feed flood, solar or line-of-sight models. The viewer bundle follows a documented, versioned contract.
  6. It gets datums right. We found that CartoDEM V3R1 heights are measured from the WGS84 ellipsoid, not sea level: at Namchi they sit about 46 m below Copernicus GLO-30, which matches the EGM2008 geoid height there (−43.8 m). DepthWizard converts CartoDEM automatically, so CartoDEM-calibrated results agree with other sea-level products.
  7. Its parts are reusable. Seamless tiled depth for very large images, DEM and datum handling, a scale-source chain with confidence labels, and a leak-proof evaluation harness are all separate modules with tests.
  8. It's open. The depth model is Apache-2.0. Evaluation uses public data. The code, the documentation and the evaluation runs are all in the repository.

Where it fits in existing workflows

Image on one screen, a 30 m DEM on another. Heights of buildings are guessed, measured in the field, or waited for.

Drop the image in, get a DSM in under a minute, fly through it, measure heights and slopes, export the GeoTIFF.

5 · How it works

The pipeline on one page

  1. Read. The image is read with rasterio. Multi-band or 16-bit images are reduced to RGB and stretched to 8-bit; images in latitude/longitude are reprojected to the local UTM zone so pixels are square and measured in metres.
  2. Relative height. Depth Anything V2 (Small) predicts relative height twice: once over the whole image at low resolution, and again on overlapping full-resolution tiles. Each tile is aligned to the whole-image pass and blended with its neighbours, so a 4096 × 4096 image comes out without seams.
  3. Calibrate. For a georeferenced image, the DEM for that area (a local file, or Copernicus GLO-30 downloaded once and cached) is resampled onto the image grid. The DSM keeps the DEM's terrain and adds the network's fine detail, scaled to metres by ground control points or by the DEM itself.
  4. Export. A full-resolution Float32 GeoTIFF with calibration tags, plus a compact bundle for the viewer (a height grid, the image, a mask of filled gaps, and metadata).
  5. Explore and check. The viewer drapes the image over the terrain: orbit or fly, click for heights, draw profiles, colour by slope, and upload a reference DSM to see the error.

6 · The technology

Inside each stage

6.1 Reading the image

  • Accepts .png .jpg .jpeg .tif .tiff. A file is georeferenced when it has a CRS and a real geotransform.
  • Bands 1–3 are taken as RGB (configurable); palette images are expanded; 16-bit or multi-band data is stretched per band between the 2nd and 98th percentiles.
  • Geographic images (EPSG:4326 and similar) are reprojected to the UTM zone of the scene centre with bilinear resampling, keeping the native ground resolution.
  • GeoTIFF nodata and alpha masks are honoured and carried through to the viewer as holes. Images longer than 12 000 px on a side are downsampled with a warning.

6.2 Relative height with Depth Anything V2

The model. Depth Anything V2 Small (depth-anything/Depth-Anything-V2-Small-hf, Apache-2.0) pairs a DINOv2 vision-transformer encoder with a DPT decoder, about 25 million parameters in all. It was trained on precise synthetic depth and then on large numbers of pseudo-labelled real photos, which makes it strong at fine detail. It predicts relative inverse depth: for a camera looking straight down, larger means closer to the camera, which means taller. We check this polarity on GAMUS height-above-ground tiles.

Why Small. It fits an 8 GB laptop GPU with room to spare, its licence allows any use (the larger checkpoints are non-commercial), and published remote-sensing work fine-tunes it successfully.

StepWhat we do
Global passResize the whole image to 1036 px on its long side (a multiple of the 14 px ViT patch), predict, upsample to full resolution. This pass sees the whole scene and fixes the large-scale shape.
Tiles518 × 518 px at native resolution, stride 259 (50 % overlap), batches of 8 in fp16. On a GPU out-of-memory error the batch size halves; on a CPU the model runs in fp32.
AlignmentEach tile's prediction t gets a scale and shift (α, β) so that α·t + β matches the global pass. The fit starts from a robust median/MAD estimate, then refits twice dropping the 5 % largest residuals, so a few odd pixels can't collapse the slope.
BlendingAligned tiles are summed under a 2D Hann (sin²) window and divided by the summed weights; the window stays flat at the image border so edges aren't darkened.
Seam checkOn a 4096 × 4096 Denver scene, the height gradient along tile borders is 0.83 times the gradient inside tiles: below 1, so there are no visible seams.

6.3 Calibration: from relative height to metres

The DEM D is resampled onto the image grid. The relative height d is split into a smooth part and a detail part with a Gaussian blur whose width matches the DEM's cell size:

DSM = D + s · dhigh,    dhigh = d − Gσ(d),    σ = k · DEM cell / pixel size

A 30 m surface DEM is roughly a low-pass version of the true surface, so the network only has to supply the high-frequency part. k = 1.0, tuned on the validation split (the physical argument gives 0.5; a wider σ would count mid-sized features twice). The scale s, in metres per unit of relative height, comes from the first source available:

SourceHow s is foundConfidenceStatus
Ground control pointsHeight above the DEM at each point against the detail there; least-squares fit. At least 3 points, CSV in image coordinates or lon/lat.highbuilt
OSM building heightsMedian ratio of tagged building heights to detail inside each footprint, at least 10 buildings.mediumdesigned, not built
DEM bandThe DEM itself resolves detail from its cell size up to a few cells. Both d and D are averaged to DEM cells and high-passed the same way; the slope of D's detail against d's is the scale, used only if they correlate (r ≥ 0.1 over at least 64 cells).mediumbuilt; the usual source
DEM reliefThe slope of a global affine fit, for very rough terrain.mediumswitched off by tuning
Scene priorA fixed height for the 98th percentile of the detail. Tuned to 0 m: when nothing else applies, the DSM is the DEM and says so.lowfallback

Why the DEM band works. Where the network and the DEM agree about the detail they can both see, the network's scale is trustworthy and it adds its finer detail. Where they disagree (flat farmland where the network invents relief, for example), the correlation gate fails and DepthWizard falls back to the DEM instead of adding noise. This is why hilly and sparse scenes come out level with the DEM rather than worse.

Method A, the baseline. Average d down to the DEM grid and fit D ≈ a·d + b with a Huber loss. Simple, and badly wrong on hills, where the network mixes up slope and height. It is reported for comparison only.

6.5 Relative mode

Without a georeference there are no metres, and DepthWizard doesn't pretend otherwise. The relative DSM is d rescaled so its 1st and 99th percentiles map to 0 and 1, exported as a GeoTIFF without a CRS. The viewer shows heights as percentages and labels slopes as uncalibrated. If the user enters a ground sample distance, horizontal distances become approximate metres, flagged as "approximate scale".

6 · The technology (continued)

6.8 The viewer

The 3D viewer is stratum, an offline Three.js terrain workspace (TypeScript, Vite, a web worker for mesh building), extended with a small, logged DepthWizard module. From stratum: orbit and pointer-lock flight (WASD), height inspection on hover and click, slope and elevation colouring, hillshade and contour lines, vertical exaggeration, elevation profiles with CSV export, and reference RMSE/MAE with an error heatmap. Added by DepthWizard: Process image (upload and progress), Terrains (switch between every result on the computer), Validate (server-side metrics at full resolution), a calibration panel, a GeoTIFF download link, percentage readouts and an "approximate scale" label for relative results.

6.9 The desktop app

A Tauri 2 window (Rust, the system WebView2) starts the backend, a PyInstaller build of the Python service with the model, the viewer and the geoid grid inside, on 127.0.0.1, and stops it when the window closes. The target machine needs no Python. Results and caches go to the user's app-data folder, so the program folder stays read-only.

It ships as a portable folder of about 4.7 GB, almost all of it PyTorch with CUDA; without a GPU it runs on the CPU. The same backend also runs as a local web app (run_local.bat).

6.11 How we keep it correct

  • Unit and integration tests for reading, tiling and stitching, calibration (synthetic scenes with known answers), datums and masks, metrics, the bundle format, the API (including corrupt uploads) and CPU-only runs.
  • A viewer contract suite (17 browser checks) loads synthetic bundles with known geometry: a 100 m cone must read 100 m at its peak and 14° on its flank, north must point the right way, relative results must show percentages, validation against itself must give zero error, and a 4096 × 4096 input must stay interactive.
  • Every setting in one config file, type-checked when loaded, and saved with every evaluation run.
  • A no-leak rule, enforced by a test: the calibration code never imports the evaluation code, so reference LiDAR can't reach the pipeline.

Why these datasets

  • An accuracy test needs matching truth. NAIP aerial images and USGS 3DEP LiDAR cover the same places, are public, and span all four landscapes the brief names. They are aerial, not satellite; we say so wherever the numbers appear.
  • GAMUS is the brief's recommended dataset: aerial RGB tiles with height above ground from three US cities. It has no georeference, so it tests relative mode (scale-aligned) and supplies training data for fine-tuning.
  • India is shown qualitatively. We found no public LiDAR for Indian cities, so Indian scenes carry no accuracy claims. With ISRO reference data, the same evaluation harness would produce them.
  • No leakage. Urban test sites are outside Washington DC, New York and Philadelphia, the GAMUS cities, so a GAMUS-fine-tuned model is never scored on the cities it trained on.

8 · How we evaluate

Numbers we can defend

Half the score is accuracy, so the evaluation is built so that we can't fool ourselves: fixed splits, tuning on validation only, the DEM-only baseline in every table, and failures shown with the same prominence as wins.

Metrics

The reference is reprojected onto the prediction grid (bilinear); only pixels valid in both count. The error is e = prediction − reference.

Offset-free RMSERMSE after removing median(e)Separates datum mismatch from shape error

Splits and rules

Splits

quick: 5 validation tiles, run after every change. val: the northern tile rows, for tuning. test: $all_b_tiles LiDAR tiles across urban, sparse, hilly and forested, plus $gamus_tiles GAMUS test tiles for relative mode.

Rules

Test tile lists never change once evaluated. Tuning uses val only. Calibration code never reads reference data. Every run saves its config, per-tile CSV, metrics and error maps.

Scene mode

Each site's whole 4096 px scene is processed once, the way a user's upload would be (depth, DEM, calibration), and the test tiles are scored inside it. A single 300–600 m tile holds only about 10 × 10 DEM cells, too few to learn a scale from; our first tuning round, done per tile, showed exactly that failure, and scene mode is the realistic fix. Calibration still never sees the reference.

Methods compared

  • DEM only: the calibration DEM resampled to the image grid. It answers "what did the model add?"
  • Method A: a global affine fit of the network output to the DEM (baseline).
  • Method B (DepthWizard): DEM terrain plus calibrated detail, the default.
  • Relative mode (GAMUS): scale-aligned, labelled as such everywhere.

Reading the results

  • Better than the DEM overall: RMSE $all_dem_rmse → $all_b_rmse m ($all_gain_rmse % lower) and correlation $all_dem_r → $all_b_r over all $all_b_tiles tiles.
  • Where the detail matters, it helps: urban $urban_dem_rmse → $urban_b_rmse m, forested $forested_dem_rmse → $forested_b_rmse m. On buildings and trees alone, sparse scenes improve most: nDSM RMSE $sparse_dem_ndsm → $sparse_b_ndsm m ($sparse_gain_ndsm % lower).
  • Where it can't help, it doesn't hurt: hilly ($hilly_dem_rmse → $hilly_b_rmse m) and sparse ($sparse_dem_rmse → $sparse_b_rmse m) come out level with the DEM, because the correlation gate falls back to the DEM when the network disagrees with the terrain.
  • The gains are modest. The network is general-purpose, trained on ground-level photos; a 30 m DEM already explains most of the height. Fine-tuning on top-down data (section 12) is the next step to widen the margin.
  • The naive approach fails. Method A, the network stretched to the DEM, scores $all_a_rmse m overall (appendix D).

How tuning changed the result

Settings were tuned on the validation split only, in scene mode (run $tune_run). The objective is the mean over landscapes of the mean per-tile RMSE:

The untuned version invented relief on flat and hilly ground and lost to the DEM. The fix wasn't a bigger model but a better scale source, the DEM band with its correlation gate, which is why the tuned pipeline beats the DEM on the test split it had never seen.

10 · Case study

Namchi, South Sikkim

A hill town on steep slopes: the DEM knows the mountain, the image knows the town. This is the demo scene, and the kind of place in India where DepthWizard is most useful.

The full 2.5 km × 2.5 km scene. Left: the WorldView-3 image. Middle: CartoDEM alone, hillshaded. Right: DepthWizard's DSM. Imagery: Maxar Open Data Program (CC BY-NC 4.0). CartoDEM Version-3 R1, National Remote Sensing Centre, ISRO, Government of India, Hyderabad, India.

The scene

ImageWorldView-3, 14 March 2022, 0.3 m, Maxar Open Data Program
Size8192 × 8192 px, UTM zone 45N (EPSG:32645)
DEMCartoDEM V3R1 tile 88E27N, converted from the WGS84 ellipsoid to EGM2008
Heightsabout 960 m to 1860 m above sea level
Timeread 1.4 s, relative height 27.3 s, calibration 5.1 s, export 5.2 s

How it was calibrated

The DEM-band source applied: the DEM's own resolvable detail and the network's correlate (r = 0.157 over 7396 DEM cells, above the 0.1 gate), giving a scale of 66 m per unit of relative detail. Confidence: medium. The whole calibration record is stored in the GeoTIFF tags and shown in the viewer's calibration panel.

A datum catch. Uncorrected, CartoDEM sat about 46 m below Copernicus GLO-30 here, matching the local EGM2008 geoid height (−43.8 m): CartoDEM V3R1 is ellipsoidal. DepthWizard now converts it automatically.

No accuracy claim. There is no public LiDAR for Namchi, so this scene is shown qualitatively. The accuracy numbers in section 9 come from the US test sites.

11 · Limitations

What it can't do yet

Stated plainly, because a DSM is only useful if you know when not to trust it.

LimitationDetail
No Indian accuracy numbersNo public LiDAR for Indian cities was found. Indian scenes are qualitative until reference data is available.
Aerial test imageryThe LiDAR test sites use NAIP aerial images (0.3–0.6 m). ISRO satellite imagery differs in resolution, haze and viewing angle.
Modest gains over the DEMThe network is general-purpose; most of the height comes from the DEM. Fine-tuning on top-down data is in progress.
Tall buildingsDowntown towers are under-restored (the Denver tiles in the gallery). In off-nadir images tall buildings also lean, so roof heights land beside their footprints.
Water and shadowsThe network's output over water is noise, and dark shadows can read as low ground.
Flat, featureless groundThe network may invent relief. The correlation gate usually falls back to the DEM, at low confidence.
Relative mode has no metresPlain photos give relative heights. A ground sample distance gives horizontal scale only.
DEM quality mattersThe DSM inherits the DEM's terrain. A bare-earth DEM under-predicts dense built-up areas.
Neighbouring splitsValidation and test tiles come from neighbouring parts of the same six sites; more sites are needed before claiming generalisation to new places.
Package sizeThe desktop build is about 4.7 GB, almost all of it PyTorch with CUDA.

Appendix C

Glossary

DSMDigital surface model: the height of the top surface, including buildings and trees.
DTM / bare-earth DEMDigital terrain model: the height of the ground with buildings and vegetation removed.
DEMDigital elevation model: the general term. SRTM, Copernicus GLO-30 and CartoDEM are surface-like DEMs at about 30 m.
nDSMNormalised DSM: DSM minus DTM, the height of objects above the ground.
Relative DSMHeights with the right order and shape but no units; scaled 0 to 1.
Absolute DSMHeights in metres above a stated vertical datum.
GeoreferencedAn image with a coordinate reference system and a transform from pixels to map coordinates.
GSDGround sample distance: the ground size of one pixel, in metres.
UTMUniversal Transverse Mercator: a map projection in metres, used so pixels are square.
Vertical datumThe surface heights are measured from: an ellipsoid (WGS84) or a geoid model (EGM2008, EGM96, NAVD88).
GeoidThe equipotential surface closest to mean sea level; EGM2008 models it worldwide.
GCPGround control point: a location with a known height.
Monocular depthEstimating depth from a single image.
ViT / DPTVision transformer encoder / Dense Prediction Transformer decoder, the architecture of Depth Anything V2.
Scale-alignedA relative prediction fitted to the reference with the best scale and shift before scoring.
RMSE, MAE, NMADRoot-mean-square error, mean absolute error, normalised median absolute deviation (see section 8).
Hann windowA smooth weighting that fades to zero at tile edges, used to blend overlapping tiles.
Huber lossA fitting loss that is quadratic for small errors and linear for large ones, so outliers count less.
